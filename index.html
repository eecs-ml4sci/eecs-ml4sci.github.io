Course description

What makes machine learning different when the goal is to understand, model, and discover the behavior of the natural world? This course provides a broad introduction to machine learning for science, an emerging area reshaping computational science, from surrogate modeling of physical systems to scientific discovery. Scientific problems bring distinctive challenges including structured data, physical laws, and multiscale dynamics that require us to rethink the representations, inductive biases, objectives, and evaluation criteria used in conventional machine learning. The course develops the foundations needed to understand these challenges and surveys modern approaches for learning scientific systems, from physics-informed models and learned operators to generative models and large-scale scientific foundation models. Throughout, we will examine not only how to build increasingly capable models, but also how to make them reliable, scalable, and ultimately useful for scientific understanding and discovery.

  • Final project: Student will conduct an original investigation, replication, extension, or comparative study in machine learning for science.

Frequently asked questions

Who is this course for?

The course is intended for advanced undergraduate and graduate students interested in applying modern machine learning to scientific and engineering problems. The undergraduate and graduate offerings share a common intellectual core, with additional expectations for graduate enrollment.

What background is expected?

Students should be comfortable with core machine learning concepts and programming. Familiarity with linear algebra, probability, and differential equations is helpful. The course will introduce the scientific computing and PDE concepts needed to motivate the methods we study.

Is this a course about AI agents or LLMs for science?

Not quite. The course focuses on the machine learning foundations of scientific modeling: representations, inductive biases, dynamics, operators, physical constraints, uncertainty, scaling, and interpretability. Scientific foundation models appear later as one part of the broader landscape.

Will the course be useful if I work outside physics?

Yes. Although PDEs and physical fields provide important running examples, the principles extend to many scientific domains, including engineering, climate, materials, biology, chemistry, and other settings involving structured data, governing constraints, dynamics, and uncertainty.

What will the final project look like?

Students will formulate and investigate a substantive question in machine learning for science. Projects may involve reproducing and critically evaluating a recent paper, extending an existing method, comparing modeling approaches, or applying modern methods to a scientific dataset.

Can we work in groups?

Students are encouraged to work in groups of 2-3.
